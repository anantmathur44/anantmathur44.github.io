Majorize–Minimize (MM) Algorithms and the Lasso

Anant Mathur · July 2026

This post is based on the paper A Fast MM Algorithm for Group Lasso by Anant Mathur, Sarat Moka and Zdravko Botev (School of Mathematics and Statistics, UNSW Sydney), currently under review at the Journal of Computational and Graphical Statistics. [PDF]

Setting: the group lasso

Suppose we are given a dataset $(\v y, \m X)$ consisting of a response vector $\v y \in \bb{R}^{n}$ and a feature matrix $\m X \in \bb{R}^{n \times p}$. In group selection, the feature matrix is decomposed into $J$ submatrices, $\m X = [\m X_1, \ldots, \m X_J]$, with $\m X_j \in \bb{R}^{n \times p_j}$ and $p_1 + \cdots + p_J = p$, and we ask which groups matter in the linear model

$$ \m Y = \beta_0 \m 1 + \m X_1 \v\beta_1 + \cdots + \m X_J \v\beta_J + \v\epsilon, \qquad \v\epsilon \sim \c{N}(\v 0, \sigma^2 \m I_n). $$

Following the standardized group lasso, each block $\m X_j$ is sphered via its QR decomposition $\m X_j = \m Z_j \m R_j$, so that $\m Z_j^\top \m Z_j = \m I_{p_j}$. Writing $\v\theta_j := \m R_j \v\beta_j$, the group lasso estimate minimizes

$$ f(\v\theta) \;:=\; \frac{1}{2n}\Big\|\v y - \sum_{j=1}^{J} \m Z_j \v\theta_j\Big\|_2^2 \;+\; \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{J} \sqrt{p_j}\,\|\v\theta_j\|_2. $$

The non-smooth penalty $\sum_j \sqrt{p_j}\,\|\v\theta_j\|_2$ sets entire groups $\widehat{\v\theta}_j$ to zero — larger $\lambda$, more groups shrunk to zero. When each group contains a single predictor this is exactly the vanilla lasso. The textbook solver is block coordinate descent (BCD): cycling over groups and applying the lasso shrinkage operator

$$ S_{\alpha_j}(\v x) := \v x\,\big[1 - \alpha_j / \|\v x\|_2\big]_+, \qquad \alpha_j := n \lambda \sqrt{p_j}, $$

to each partial residual. BCD is simple, but it converges slowly when $\m X$ is poorly conditioned or when the solution is not highly sparse — precisely the regimes that motivate a different algorithmic principle.

The MM principle

A majorize–minimize (MM) algorithm minimizes an objective $g(\v\gamma)$ by successively minimizing a simpler surrogate $h(\v\gamma, \widetilde{\v\gamma})$, where $\widetilde{\v\gamma}$ is the current iterate. The surrogate must majorize $g$:

$$ g(\widetilde{\v\gamma}) = h(\widetilde{\v\gamma}, \widetilde{\v\gamma}), \qquad g(\v\gamma) \le h(\v\gamma, \widetilde{\v\gamma}) \quad \text{for all } \v\gamma. $$

In words: $h(\cdot, \widetilde{\v\gamma})$ lies above $g$ everywhere and touches it at $\v\gamma = \widetilde{\v\gamma}$. With the MM map $M(\cdot) := \argmin_{\v\gamma} h(\v\gamma, \cdot)$, the update $\v\gamma^{(t+1)} \leftarrow M(\v\gamma^{(t)})$ guarantees the descent property:

$$ g(\v\gamma^{(t+1)}) \;\le\; h(\v\gamma^{(t+1)}, \v\gamma^{(t)}) \;\le\; h(\v\gamma^{(t)}, \v\gamma^{(t)}) \;=\; g(\v\gamma^{(t)}). $$

The first inequality is majorization, the second holds because $\v\gamma^{(t+1)}$ minimizes the surrogate, and the equality is tangency. Every iteration drives the objective downhill — no step sizes, no line searches. The art of MM lies entirely in constructing a surrogate that is tight enough to make fast progress yet simple enough to minimize in closed form (ideally one that separates the variables so each coordinate updates independently).

MM for the group lasso

Rather than attacking $f(\v\theta)$ directly, the paper reparameterizes the problem with auxiliary variables $\v\gamma = [\gamma_1, \ldots, \gamma_J]^\top \in \bb{R}_+^J$ — one non-negative scalar per group — reducing the optimization from $\bb{R}^p$ to $\bb{R}_+^J$. Defining

$$ \m G(\v\gamma) := \Big(\m I + \sum_{j=1}^{J} \gamma_j \m Z_j \m Z_j^\top\Big)^{-1}, $$

the auxiliary objective is

$$ g(\v\gamma) := \v y^\top \m G(\v\gamma)\, \v y + (\v\alpha \odot \v\alpha)^\top \v\gamma, $$

where $\v\alpha = [\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_J]^\top$ and $\odot$ is the element-wise product. The key fact (Theorem 3.1 in the paper) is that the first-order conditions for minimizing $g$ over $\bb{R}_+^J$ are equivalent to the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions of the original group lasso: from a minimizer $\widehat{\v\gamma}$ one recovers $\widehat{\v\theta}_j = \widehat{\gamma}_j \m Z_j^\top \m G(\widehat{\v\gamma})\,\v y$, and the activated set is exactly $\c{A} = \{j : \widehat{\gamma}_j > 0\}$.

A surrogate for $g$ comes from a matrix version of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. Given a probability vector $\v w$ of weights, majorizing $\v y^\top \m G(\v\gamma)\, \v y$ yields a surrogate $h_{\v w}(\v\gamma, \widetilde{\v\gamma})$ that separates the variables $\gamma_1, \ldots, \gamma_J$, so each group updates in closed form:

$$ \gamma_j^{(t+1)} \;\leftarrow\; S_{\alpha_j w_j}\!\Big( (w_j + \gamma_j^{(t)})\, \tau_j^{(t)} \Big) \Big/ \alpha_j, \qquad \tau_j^{(t)} := \big\|\m Z_j^\top \m G(\v\gamma^{(t)})\, \v y\big\|_2. $$

The same lasso shrinkage operator $S$ reappears — but now all $J$ groups are updated concurrently from a single linear solve $\m G(\v\gamma^{(t)})\,\v y$, computed cheaply with a few conjugate-gradient steps over only the activated groups. Because any valid probability vector $\v w$ gives a legitimate majorization, the weights can be chosen adaptively at each iteration to prioritize the groups furthest from satisfying the KKT conditions — this is what accelerates convergence.

Why it matters

The MM algorithm converges globally to the group-lasso KKT point, and in simulations and real-data experiments it converges up to an order of magnitude faster than BCD when computed over a regularization path — with the largest gains exactly where BCD struggles: poorly conditioned features and solutions with many activated groups. For the optimal weight selection, the convergence theory, and the numerical experiments, see the full paper.
