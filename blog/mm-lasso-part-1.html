The idea

A majorize–minimize (MM) algorithm turns a hard minimization problem into a sequence of easy ones. The principle traces back to the optimization-transfer ideas surveyed and popularised in the seminal work of Kenneth Lange and collaborators — see Lange, Hunter & Yang (2000), the widely read tutorial by Hunter & Lange (2004), Lange's monograph MM Optimization Algorithms (SIAM, 2016), and Lange's lecture slides from the Jan de Leeuw workshop at UCLA — de Leeuw being one of the earliest practitioners of majorization methods.

Suppose we want to minimize an objective $g(\v\gamma)$. Instead of attacking $g$ directly, at each iteration we build a surrogate function $h(\v\gamma, \widetilde{\v\gamma})$ around the current iterate $\widetilde{\v\gamma}$ satisfying the two majorization conditions

$$ g(\widetilde{\v\gamma}) = h(\widetilde{\v\gamma}, \widetilde{\v\gamma}), \qquad g(\v\gamma) \le h(\v\gamma, \widetilde{\v\gamma}) \quad \text{for all } \v\gamma . $$

That is, $h(\cdot, \widetilde{\v\gamma})$ lies above $g$ everywhere and touches it at $\v\gamma = \widetilde{\v\gamma}$. Defining the MM map $M(\cdot) := \argmin_{\v\gamma} h(\v\gamma, \cdot)$, the update $\v\gamma^{(t+1)} \leftarrow M(\v\gamma^{(t)})$ enjoys the descent property:

$$ g(\v\gamma^{(t+1)}) \;\le\; h(\v\gamma^{(t+1)}, \v\gamma^{(t)}) \;\le\; h(\v\gamma^{(t)}, \v\gamma^{(t)}) \;=\; g(\v\gamma^{(t)}). $$

The first inequality is majorization, the second holds because $\v\gamma^{(t+1)}$ minimizes the surrogate, and the equality is tangency. Every iteration drives the objective downhill — no step sizes, no line searches. The art lies in choosing a surrogate tight enough to make fast progress yet simple enough to minimize in closed form.

Watch it work

Below, the orange curve is a non-convex objective $g$. At each step the current surrogate — a quadratic majorizer $h(\cdot, \widetilde\gamma)$ (peach) — touches $g$ at the current iterate and lies above it everywhere. Minimizing the parabola gives the next iterate, and the process repeats, sliding downhill.

Quadratic majorization $h(\gamma,\widetilde\gamma) = g(\widetilde\gamma) + g'(\widetilde\gamma)(\gamma - \widetilde\gamma) + \tfrac{L}{2}(\gamma-\widetilde\gamma)^2$ with $L \ge \sup g''$. Each minimization gives $\gamma^{(t+1)} = \widetilde\gamma - g'(\widetilde\gamma)/L$.

Classical examples

Many workhorse algorithms in statistics are MM algorithms in disguise. The EM algorithm of Dempster, Laird & Rubin (1977) is the canonical special case: the E-step constructs a minorizer of the log-likelihood via Jensen's inequality, and the M-step maximizes it. In multidimensional scaling, de Leeuw's (1977) SMACOF algorithm majorizes the stress function — one of the earliest systematic uses of majorization. For logistic regression, Böhning & Lindsay (1988) bound the Hessian by a fixed matrix to obtain a monotonic quadratic-bound algorithm. And quantile regression admits an elegant MM treatment via perturbed absolute-value majorizers (Hunter & Lange, 2000).

In the rest of this series we build an MM algorithm for a modern problem: the (group) lasso. Part 2 sets up the lasso and its optimality conditions.
